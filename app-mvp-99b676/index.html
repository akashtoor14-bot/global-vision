01 The premise

Everything at once, because a half-switch is worse than either state

Version one is the whole ecosystem. The member app, the desktop site, the CRM, the admin portal and the IB portal all go live together, and on that day nothing depends on GoHighLevel, Telegram, Discord or Messenger.

That is a deliberate choice and a defensible one. The alternative — shipping in slices — means running the old platforms and the new ones in parallel for months: clients in two CRMs, admins in two chat tools, conversation history split across systems, and every person involved having to know which world they are in for any given task. The cost of that is real and it lands on the people least able to absorb it.

The consequence is that the launch date becomes the dependent variable. Scope is fixed by this decision and quality cannot be the thing that gives, so the date has to be derived from the work rather than set in advance and worked back from. A date chosen first, with this scope, would only be met by quietly cutting something — which is the outcome this decision exists to prevent.

What this document does, therefore, is not argue for cuts. It states the full scope plainly, orders the build, and defines readiness precisely enough that "are we ready" has an answer rather than an opinion.

02 The critical path

Shipping together does not mean building together

One launch date does not make the work parallel. The pieces depend on each other, and that order does not change because everything arrives on the same day.

The CRM is still first, and for the same reason as before: access to the app is gated on verification inside the CRM. On launch day there must already be a working CRM with verified clients in it, or nobody can log in. Not fewer people — nobody. The CRM is not the back office to be built once the app is done; it is what the app is waiting on.

04 What one launch costs

The decision is sound; the bill for it should be visible

Shipping everything together avoids a long, confusing parallel-running period. It buys that by concentrating every risk onto a single day, and it removes the two things that normally make a launch survivable.

There is no learning from a smaller launch first

A staged rollout is partly a scope decision and partly an information-gathering one: the first slice teaches you how the migration behaves, how members react, where support load lands, what nobody thought of. Launching everything at once means every one of those lessons arrives at the same time, in production, with the old platforms already off. The substitute is rehearsal — the migration run against real data more than once (C11.3), and cohort migration on the day (C11.9) so a fault meets a hundred records rather than all of them.

There is no fallback unless one is deliberately kept

"We do not have to rely on the old platforms" is the goal, and it is achieved the moment the new ones work. That is not the same as deleting the old ones. Every retiring platform should be left dormant and readable rather than cancelled, for a defined period after launch. It costs a subscription; it buys the ability to recover from a discovery made in week three. Cancelling on launch day converts an inconvenience into an emergency.

The date is derived, not chosen

With scope fixed and quality not available as a variable, the date is whatever the work takes. That is the honest arithmetic and it is worth stating to the team in advance, because the alternative is a date set early, missed, and then met by cutting something quietly — which is precisely what this decision was made to avoid.

Depth is still a lever, and it is not a cut

Every requirement ships. How complete each one is on day one is a separate question, and it is the remaining control on the schedule. A benefits module that lists entitlements, takes a receipt upload and routes it for manual approval satisfies W4 — automated entitlement calculation can follow without anyone being blocked (W4.7). The same reasoning applies wherever a manual step is tolerable at launch volume. Deciding this deliberately, in writing, before the build starts is very different from discovering it under deadline pressure.

05 Sequence

Build order, all landing before one date

The first two stages produce nothing anyone can see. That is the correct order anyway, and worth defending when it starts to feel slow.

M1
Client record and audit trail

The data model, individual admin logins, server-side access scoping, and logging from the first write. Everything attaches to this. Building it second means losing history permanently and revisiting every query later.

M2
Broker data and verification

The MT5 and broker feed, deposit confirmation, activity tracking, pipelines. Milestone: the first client verified end to end. The first moment the platform does something real.

M3
Access bridge

Verified record becomes an app login. Broker UID, locked sponsor, multiple MT5 accounts, the 30-day rule enforced in both directions.

M4
App and desktop

Desks, Tap to Trade, journal, community, Academy, Vision AI, and the desktop site. Largely specified already. R1 must be resolved before this ships and does not move faster because engineering is ready.

M5
Admin portal

Attendance, leave, contracts, benefits, KPIs, chat and calling, training. Runs parallel to M3 and M4 — it shares almost no code with the client path, so sequencing it after them would add its full duration to the schedule for no reason.

M6
IB portal

Scoped views, verification participation, sub-IB admins, support routing. Depends on M1 scoping and M2 pipelines, and can run alongside M4 once those exist.

M7
Migration rehearsal

Export everything from GoHighLevel, Telegram history where it matters, Discord, and Messenger. Map it, load it, reconcile. Then do it again. A full stage, not a task inside launch week. The first rehearsal is where you find what does not map; finding that during cut-over is the failure this document exists to prevent. The Academy destination (C11.7) must be settled before this stage can finish.

M8
Cut-over and launch

Re-verify the community, migrate in cohorts, move members across, retire all four platforms. Old systems go dormant and readable, not cancelled. The riskiest stage, and the only one no amount of internal testing fully rehearses.

M5 and M6 are the reason this is not simply a longer version of the old plan. Both run in parallel with the client path rather than after it. Whether that is possible depends on how many people can work at once, which is the question that actually determines the launch date — not the requirement count.

06 What launch ready means

Written in advance, so the decision is not an opinion on the day

With no staged rollout to learn from, this checklist is the only thing standing between a considered launch and an optimistic one. Every line is pass or fail.

The client path

  • A new client goes from first contact to trading in the app without anyone touching a database by hand.
  • Every existing member has been re-verified, or knows exactly what they must do and by when.
  • The 30-day rule has been tested in both directions: revoking an idle account, and restoring one.
  • The broker feed has run for a full month without a silent failure. A failure here revokes access from active traders — the worst outcome in either document.
  • Tap to Trade has been run on demo accounts by people who did not build it.
  • R1 is closed, in writing, by somebody qualified to close it.
  • Every member feature works on desktop, verified by using it rather than by assuming parity.

The migration

  • Run against real data and reconciled at least twice, with the reports kept.
  • Every client, conversation and course exists in the new system and can be found there.
  • All four retiring platforms are still readable, and nobody has cancelled an account.
  • The Academy courses have a working home and members can reach them.

The team

  • Every admin has their own login and has been trained on the verification path.
  • Admins can clock in, request leave, see their contract and file a claim — what they will do in week one.
  • Admin chat, voice and video work well enough that nobody reopens Discord out of habit.
  • Claim receipts are encrypted, out of search and exports, with a working deletion schedule — not a planned one.
  • No admin can see another admin’s contract, pay or claims. Verified by trying it, not by reading the code.
  • An IB can see their own clients and, tested deliberately, cannot see anybody else’s.

The day itself

  • The rollback plan is written: what happens if this has to be reversed, who decides, and where members are told to go.
  • Support cover is arranged for launch week specifically, at a level nobody would sustain permanently.
  • Somebody owns the decision to stop. A launch with no defined abort condition does not have one.

07 What could break this

Six things, in the order they would hurt

A single-date, four-platform switch with no parallel running has a specific risk profile. These are not generic project risks; they are the ones this delivery mode creates.

L1
The migration loses something nobody notices until later

Four platforms are switched off, so anything not carried across is gone. The dangerous failures are quiet: a custom field nobody mapped, conversation history that arrived truncated, membership state that passed a spot check. Mitigation: rehearse twice against real data, reconcile by count rather than by eye, migrate in cohorts, and keep every old platform readable afterwards so a late discovery is recoverable rather than fatal.

L2
The date is set before the scope is costed

The most likely failure of a fixed-scope launch is a date picked for external reasons and then met by quietly dropping something. That converts a deliberate decision into an accidental one. Mitigation: derive the date from the sequence in section 05 after the parallelism question is answered, and treat any proposal to move it as a scope conversation rather than a scheduling one.

L3
Four replacements land at once and the weakest one decides the verdict

People judge a switch by whichever part serves them worst. An excellent app will not stop admins going back to Discord if portal chat is poor, and once a team has returned to a familiar tool, moving them a second time is much harder than the first. Mitigation: keep old platforms dormant rather than deleted, and treat somebody reopening an old tool as a defect report rather than a discipline problem. It is the earliest signal available.

L4
Verification does not scale on launch day

Every existing member needs re-verifying at once. At ten minutes per client, community size decides how long launch takes, and it is likely to be weeks rather than days. Mitigation: measure the real per-client time during M2, not on launch day, and stage the community in cohorts.

L5
Scope grows because everything is already in scope

Paradoxically, a no-cuts launch is easier to add to than a staged one: with nothing deferred there is no obvious category for a new idea except "in". The admin portal is most exposed, because everyone using it has a personal stake in one of its features. Mitigation: a dated scope freeze, after which additions are post-launch by default rather than debated individually on merit.

L6
R1 arrives late and blocks a finished product

Tap to Trade is the flagship and carries regulatory exposure. Legal review does not accelerate because engineering is done, and with a single launch date it can hold the entire ecosystem rather than one feature. Mitigation: start R1 now, during M1, not when M4 is ready.
